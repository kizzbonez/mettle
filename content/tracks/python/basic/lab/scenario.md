The Riverside Community Choir rehearses every Thursday evening in a church hall. To plan seating,
order music copies and report numbers for its arts grant, the choir needs to know how many
singers came to each rehearsal, section by section.

Counting is harder than it sounds. Singers drift in late after work and slip out early to catch a
bus, so the volunteer with the register walks round a few times during the evening and writes
down a headcount for each section. The choir's rule is simple: a section's attendance is the
**largest number counted at one time**, not the total of all the counts. If two altos are there
at half past seven and three at eight, the altos score three, because the first two were almost
certainly among the three.

The volunteer hands in the notes as plain text, one count per line:

```text
# Thursday rehearsal, Riverside hall
altos, 2
Basses, 1
altos, 3
tenors, four
```

The notes are typed in a hurry. Section names come in any mix of capitals and with stray
spaces, counts are sometimes written as words, and the odd line has no count at all. Last term
the treasurer added up every count by hand and reported forty tenors in a choir that has nine.

You are writing the script that does it properly: it reads one rehearsal's notes, explains
exactly which lines it could not use and why, so the volunteer can fix them, and prints the
rehearsal's attendance.
